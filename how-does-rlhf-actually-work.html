Alignment

How does RLHF actually work, and what does it change?

November 10, 2026 Adele Chinda 12 min read

Originally published on Medium on November 10, 2026. Read the original at medium.com/@chindahel1.

GPT-3 was already an impressive model when it arrived in 2020. Given a prompt, it could write essays, translate languages, and answer questions. But if you asked it a question, it was as likely to continue with another question as to answer yours. ChatGPT, built on the same family of models, answered directly, admitted uncertainty, and refused when asked for something harmful. The difference between the two was not more pretraining data or a bigger model. It was a post-training recipe called RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback.

RLHF is the process that turns a raw next-token predictor into an assistant. It is also one of the most misunderstood parts of modern language model training. This post walks through the mechanics, stage by stage, and then asks the question researchers still argue about: does RLHF actually teach a model anything new, or does it just dress up what pretraining already put there?

The problem: a model that completes, not a model that helps

Pretraining teaches a model one thing: predict the next token. Given a large corpus of internet text, the model learns to assign high probability to the continuations that look most like what a human would have written. That is a powerful skill, but it is not the same as being helpful.

Ask a raw pretrained model “What is the capital of France?” and a plausible completion is “What is the capital of Germany?” The model is not answering. It is continuing a pattern, and a list of questions is a perfectly good pattern. Ask it to write a polite email, and it may write three more emails, or a critique of the first one. The model has no notion of your intent. It only knows what text usually comes next.

Supervised fine-tuning, the first stage of RLHF, fixes the most visible part of this problem. But it cannot fix the deeper one, which is that “helpful” is a judgment, not a fact. There is no dataset of correct answers to train on, because for most prompts there is no single correct answer. There are only answers people prefer. RLHF is a way to train on preferences instead of on correct answers.

Stage 1: supervised fine-tuning

The first stage is the simplest. Human annotators write example prompts and the ideal responses to them, thousands of them, covering instructions, questions, coding tasks, and conversation. The pretrained model is then fine-tuned on these examples with ordinary supervised learning: given the prompt, predict the response token by token.

After this stage, the model has learned the format of an assistant. It answers instead of continuing, it structures its output, and it follows the style of the demonstrations. This stage is doing a lot of heavy lifting, and it is worth remembering that. Many of the behaviors people attribute to RLHF, like polite formatting and structured answers, are already present after supervised fine-tuning.

But supervised fine-tuning has a ceiling. It can only teach the model to imitate the demonstrations it was shown. It cannot teach the model which of two plausible answers is better, because the training signal is just “produce this exact text.” It also inherits every flaw in the demonstrations. If the annotators wrote verbose answers, the model learns to be verbose. RLHF exists to go beyond imitation.

Stage 2: the reward model

The second stage is where human preferences enter. The fine-tuned model is given a prompt and generates two or more different responses. Human annotators rank them, or simply pick the better one. Nobody writes a score. They just say: this answer is better than that one.

Those pairwise comparisons become the training data for a second model, the reward model. It is usually a copy of the language model with its output head replaced by a single number, a score. The loss pushes the gap between the two scores up. It never says what a good answer is in absolute terms. It only learns the direction of preference.

There is a reason preferences are collected as comparisons rather than as scores. Asking a person to rate an answer from 1 to 10 is noisy: one annotator’s 7 is another’s 5, and the same annotator drifts over a long session. Asking which of two answers is better is a much more reliable judgment, and it is easier to do consistently. The reward model is, in effect, a distillation of many small comparative judgments into a function that can score any answer instantly.

Once trained, the reward model is a stand-in for the human raters. It can score millions of responses without anyone in the loop. That scalability is the whole point. Human feedback is expensive and slow, so RLHF uses humans to train a judge, and then lets the judge do the work at scale.

The catch is that the judge is imperfect. It is a model trained on a finite set of comparisons, and it has blind spots. Anything that comes next inherits those blind spots, which is why the third stage needs a leash.

Stage 3: reinforcement learning with PPO

In the third stage, the language model itself becomes a reinforcement learning agent, called the policy. It is given a prompt, generates a response, and the reward model scores it. The policy’s weights are updated to produce higher-scoring responses, using an algorithm called Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO.

KL is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, a measure of how different two probability distributions are. The penalty is the leash: improve your score, but do not wander far from sensible language.

The leash exists because of reward hacking. Left unchecked, reinforcement learning will find the easiest way to maximize the reward, and the easiest way is usually not what a human wants. A policy can learn to produce text that the reward model loves but that is gibberish to a person: repeated flattery, strange tokens, confident nonsense, or answers that are long because the reward model learned that longer answers tended to be preferred. The reward model is only a proxy for human judgment, and reinforcement learning is ruthlessly good at exploiting proxies. The KL penalty keeps the policy in the region where the reward model’s judgments are still reliable.

PPO itself is worth a brief note, because it is the source of much of RLHF’s reputation for being hard to run. It is an on-policy algorithm, which means it must generate fresh responses from the current policy at every training step, score them, and then update. That requires holding the policy, the reference model, the reward model, and often a value model in memory at once, and the loop is famously sensitive to hyperparameters. This engineering cost is the main reason the field has been moving toward simpler alternatives.

The shortcut: DPO

In 2023, Rafailov and colleagues showed that the whole reward model and reinforcement learning apparatus could be skipped. Their method, Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, starts from a mathematical observation: the KL-constrained reward maximization in stage 3 has a closed-form optimal policy, and that policy can be rewritten in terms of the reward. It is a single supervised-style objective on the same preference pairs the reward model would have used.

DPO is simpler, more stable, and far cheaper to run, and on many benchmarks it matches or beats PPO-based RLHF. It is now the default preference method in many open models. Meta’s Llama 3, for example, used DPO rather than PPO for its instruction-tuned versions. But the simplification comes with a tradeoff. DPO trains only on the fixed set of preference pairs, while PPO explores: it generates new responses, gets them scored, and can in principle discover answers that no human ever demonstrated. Whether that exploration matters in practice is an open question, and it is one reason the strongest labs still run RL in some form.

Two views of what RLHF actually does

Here is the debate. One view says RLHF, and alignment more broadly, is mostly superficial. The argument, made crisply in the LIMA paper by Zhou and colleagues, is that almost all of a model’s knowledge and capability comes from pretraining, and alignment training mostly teaches style and format: how to present an answer, when to refuse, how to structure a response. On this view, RLHF does not make the model smarter. It makes it behave like an assistant. The evidence is suggestive. LIMA showed that fine-tuning a strong base model on just 1,000 carefully chosen examples produced responses competitive with models trained on far more alignment data. If alignment were teaching deep new skills, a thousand examples should not be enough.

The other view says that misses what reinforcement learning can do. When the reward is not a learned model of human preference but a verifiable signal, like whether a piece of code passes its tests or a math answer is correct, RL can push a model to genuinely better reasoning. The most striking recent example is DeepSeek-R1, which used reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to produce a model that reasons far better than its supervised-only counterpart, discovering behaviors like self-correction and extended chains of thought that were not in its training data. On this view, RL is not just a style layer. It is a way to elicit capabilities that pretraining put in the model but that imitation alone cannot surface.

Both views have evidence, and the honest summary is that it depends on the reward. When the reward is a learned model of human taste, RLHF mostly reshapes behavior toward what people like, and it inherits the reward model’s blind spots, including a tendency to reward confident, fluent, slightly longer answers. When the reward is grounded in correctness, RL can genuinely improve reasoning. The interesting research question, and the one that matters most for the next generation of models, is how to get more of the second kind of reward: signals that are verifiable, hard to game, and aligned with what we actually want the model to do.

Why this matters

RLHF matters because it is where a model’s behavior is decided. Pretraining determines what a model can do. Post-training determines what it will do. The same base model can become a helpful assistant, a terse coder, or a sycophant, depending on the preference data and the reward it was trained against. That makes the details of alignment, who writes the preferences, what the reward model learns, how strong the KL leash is, into design decisions with real consequences for millions of users.

It also matters because the current recipe is unstable. PPO-based RLHF is expensive and finicky, DPO is simpler but does not explore, and reward hacking is a standing problem whenever the reward is a learned proxy. The field is actively searching for the next recipe: RL with verifiable rewards, methods that train on AI-generated feedback, and approaches that make the reward itself more reliable. If you are building or studying language models, the alignment stage is where the most consequential open questions live. The model learns language from the internet. It learns how to behave from us, and how well that works depends on how carefully we teach it.
