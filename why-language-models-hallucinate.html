Why language models hallucinate

November 3, 2026 Adele Chinda 10 min read

Originally published on Medium on November 3, 2026. Read the original at medium.com/@chindahel1.

Ask a language model a question it cannot answer, and it rarely says “I don’t know.” It answers anyway, fluently, confidently, and sometimes completely wrong. It invents a citation, a date, a name, or a fact that sounds exactly right until you check it. This behavior has a name, hallucination, and it is one of the most misunderstood properties of modern language models. It is not a bug bolted onto an otherwise reliable system. It is a direct consequence of how these models are built.

To understand why, you have to start with what a language model actually does. It does not look things up. It does not check a database before it speaks. It predicts the next token, one piece of text at a time, choosing the continuation that is most probable given everything that came before. That is the whole job, and everything else, including the appearance of knowledge, emerges from it.

Prediction is not retrieval

When a model answers a factual question correctly, it is not recalling a fact in the human sense. During training, it saw millions of sentences, and its weights adjusted so that certain patterns of text became likely continuations of other patterns. Ask it about the capital of France, and “Paris” is the most probable continuation because that pairing appeared over and over in the training data. The model is not consulting a stored fact. It is reproducing a statistical regularity.

This works remarkably well for facts that are common, consistent, and repeated in many contexts. It breaks down in exactly the situations where a human would hesitate. Rare facts appear few times in training, so the model’s estimate of the right continuation is weak. Contradictory facts appear in different contexts, so the model averages over them. And questions about things that never appeared in training, like a paper published last week or a person who does not exist, have no correct continuation for the model to find. In all three cases, the model still has to produce something, because that is what it was trained to do: always produce the next token.

The crucial detail is that the model has no built in sense of its own uncertainty that matches ours. It does produce probabilities over possible next tokens, and sometimes those probabilities are spread out, which is a signal of uncertainty. But the text it generates does not carry that signal. When you sample the single most likely continuation at each step, a shaky guess and a certain fact look identical on the page. Both come out as clean, grammatical sentences. Fluency is not a signal of truth. It is a signal that the model is good at producing plausible text, and plausible text is often wrong.

Why hallucinations sound so convincing

A hallucination is rarely random gibberish. It is usually a coherent, well structured answer that borrows the shape of a correct one. A fabricated citation has a plausible author, a plausible venue, and a plausible year. A fabricated historical claim fits the style of a history paragraph. This happens because the model has learned the form of these things very well, even when it has not learned the content.

Think of it this way. The model knows what a citation looks like, what a biography paragraph looks like, and what an explanation of a scientific result looks like. When it needs to fill in the details and does not have them, it fills them in with the most probable details given the form. The result reads like a real answer because the structure is real. Only the specifics are invented.

This is also why hallucinations get worse when a question is specific and confident. If you ask a vague question, the model can give a vague answer, and vague answers are hard to get wrong. If you ask for a specific name, date, or number, there is exactly one right answer and many plausible wrong ones. The model must commit to one, and the pressure to sound specific pushes it toward a confident guess rather than a hedged one. Training with human feedback can make this worse: models are often rewarded for giving helpful, complete sounding answers, and penalized, implicitly, for refusing or hedging. The system learns that a confident answer is a good answer, even when the content is wrong.

There is a second, subtler driver. Language models are trained on text written by humans, and human text is full of confident assertions. Training data rarely contains sentences like “I am not sure about this, so I will stop here.” It contains finished articles, confident explanations, and polished arguments. The model imitates that style. It does not imitate doubt, because doubt is rarely written down in the form it was trained on.

Two views of the problem

Researchers disagree about how deep the problem runs, and the disagreement matters for what fixes are worth pursuing.

One view treats hallucination as a knowledge gap. On this view, the model hallucinates because it does not know enough, and the fix is more knowledge: bigger models, more training data, or retrieval systems that let the model look up facts before answering. Retrieval augmented generation, where the model is given relevant documents and asked to answer from them, is the most successful version of this idea. When the model has the right document in context, its answers get much more accurate, because the task shifts from recalling a fact to reading one. The limitation is that retrieval only helps when the right document exists and is found. For questions with no clear source, or where the retrieved documents are misleading, the model can still go wrong, now with a citation attached.

The other view treats hallucination as a structural property of prediction itself. On this view, no amount of knowledge fully fixes the problem, because a model that must always produce a next token will always be able to produce a wrong one, and because some questions have no answer in any document. The fix, on this view, is not just better knowledge but better calibration: models that can say “I don’t know,” that can express uncertainty in their answers, and that can refuse when the evidence is thin. This is harder than it sounds, because teaching a model to abstain requires training data and rewards that value honesty over helpfulness, and those two goals are often in tension. A model that refuses too often is useless. A model that never refuses is untrustworthy.

Both views have evidence behind them. Retrieval clearly reduces hallucinations on questions with documented answers. Calibration research clearly shows that models can be trained to express uncertainty more honestly. The honest summary is that hallucination has more than one cause, so it will need more than one fix.

How to read a model’s answer

None of this means language models are useless for factual questions. It means they should be read the way you would read a confident stranger, not the way you would read an encyclopedia.

First, check the kind of claim. Broad, widely repeated facts are usually safe. Specific names, dates, numbers, and citations are where hallucinations cluster. If an answer hinges on one specific detail, verify that detail independently.

Second, notice whether the answer could have been generated without knowing anything. A summary that restates your question in polished language, or an explanation that sounds general enough to fit many cases, may contain no real information at all. Fluency is cheap. Specificity that checks out is the real signal.

Third, use the model for what it is good at: drafting, brainstorming, explaining, and reformatting. For those tasks, a plausible answer is a useful starting point, and errors are easy to catch. For tasks where a single wrong fact is costly, like medical, legal, or safety critical decisions, the model is a drafting assistant, not an authority.

Why this matters

Hallucination matters because it sits at the boundary between capability and trust. Language models are becoming infrastructure: they draft documents, answer customer questions, summarize research, and help write code. In all of these roles, a confident wrong answer is more dangerous than an obvious error, because it passes unnoticed. The more fluent models become, the harder it gets to tell the difference between a model that knows and a model that is guessing well.

The research direction that follows from this is not just “make models bigger.” It is to build systems that know what they do not know, that can show their sources, and that can separate a confident answer from a guess in a way the user can see. Until then, the most important skill for anyone using these models is a simple one: treat fluency as style, not evidence, and check the specifics. The model will always answer. Whether the answer is true is a question only evidence can settle.
