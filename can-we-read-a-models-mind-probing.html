Can we read a model's mind? What probing actually tells us (and what it does not)

October 29, 2026 Adele Chinda 10 min read

Originally published on Medium on October 29, 2026. Read the original at medium.com/@chindahel1.

When researchers say a model “knows” something, what do they mean? Usually they mean something more specific than the model getting the right answer. They mean that somewhere inside the model, in the numbers it passes from layer to layer, there is information about that thing, and that the model uses it. Probing is the main tool for checking that claim. It is also one of the most misunderstood tools in interpretability.

A probe is a small classifier trained on a model’s hidden states. You feed the model many inputs, record the activations at some layer, and train the probe to predict a property from those activations. If the probe predicts well, you conclude the information is present in that layer. If it predicts poorly, you conclude it is absent. That sounds straightforward. It is not.

What a probe actually measures

Start with what the probe sees. A modern language model turns each input token into a vector, and each layer transforms those vectors. By the middle layers, a single vector can carry information about the word itself, the words around it, the sentence structure, and sometimes facts about the world. A probe asks whether a simple function can extract one specific property from that vector.

The key word is simple. Probes are deliberately kept small, often just a linear classifier. The reasoning is that if the information can be read out with a simple function, then it is represented in a usable form, not just hidden somewhere in the numbers. If you allowed an arbitrarily powerful probe, it could learn the property itself from correlations, and you would learn nothing about the model.

Even with that restriction, a positive probe result is a weak claim. It says the information is present and linearly readable at that layer. It does not say the model uses it. A model can carry information it ignores. Your blood test can reveal what you ate for lunch, but that does not mean your lunch caused your decisions that afternoon. Presence is not use.

A negative probe result is even weaker than it looks. It says a linear probe could not read the property from that layer. It does not say the information is absent. It might be encoded nonlinearly, spread across several vectors, or present in a form the probe cannot reach. Failing to find something with one tool is not proof it is not there.

The three questions probing cannot answer alone

This is why careful interpretability work treats probing as the start of an investigation, not the conclusion. A probe result leaves three questions open, and each needs a different method.

First, is the information causally used?

To answer that, you have to intervene. Change the hidden state in the direction the probe identifies, or remove that information, and see whether the model’s output changes in the expected way. If editing the representation changes the answer, the information was doing work. If the answer stays the same, the probe found a passenger, not a driver. This distinction matters because models are full of passengers. Representations pick up correlations from training data that never influence the final output.

Second, is the probe reading the model or the data?

A probe trained on sentences about Paris will learn to predict “France” from activations that mention Paris, but the probe might be exploiting a shortcut in the dataset rather than a fact the model computed. Good probe design controls for this by testing on held-out patterns, using minimal pairs that differ only in the property of interest, and checking whether the probe still works when the surface cues are removed. A probe that only works on easy cases is a statement about the dataset, not the model.

Third, at what point does the information appear, and what happens to it later?

Probing layer by layer gives a profile: the property may be absent early, appear in the middle layers, and vanish again near the output. That profile is useful, but it is easy to overread. Information can be transformed rather than lost. A model might convert a syntactic property into a semantic decision, so the probe stops finding syntax not because the model forgot it, but because it no longer needs it in that form. Disappearance is not the same as irrelevance.

Why probes still matter, despite all this

Given these limits, why do researchers keep probing? Because probes are cheap, fast, and surprisingly diagnostic when used as part of a larger toolkit.

Probes tell you where to look. If a property is linearly readable at layer 12 but not at layer 4, an intervention study has a concrete place to start. Without the probe, you would be searching blind through dozens of layers and thousands of dimensions.

Probes also give you a baseline for comparison. Train the same probe on a randomly initialized model, or on shuffled labels, and you get a control. If the trained model’s probe beats the control by a wide margin, something real happened during training. If it does not, the property may be an artifact of the architecture or the input itself, present before any learning took place. Controls turn a suggestive result into a meaningful one.

And probes scale to questions that are hard to study any other way. You can probe hundreds of properties across every layer of a model in an afternoon, and use the pattern of results to form hypotheses. Those hypotheses then get tested with interventions, which are slower and more targeted. Probing is the survey; intervention is the experiment.

What a good probing study looks like

If you read a paper that uses probing, here is a short checklist for judging it.

Does it use simple probes with controls? A linear probe compared against a random model and shuffled labels is the minimum. Without controls, a high accuracy number means very little.

Does it go beyond presence to use? The strongest papers follow a probe result with an intervention: edit or erase the probed direction and report what happens to the output. A probe alone is a claim about representation. A probe plus an intervention is a claim about mechanism.

Does it test generalization? A probe that only works on the training distribution may be memorizing dataset quirks. The convincing version works on new sentences, new entities, or new phrasings of the same property.

Does it avoid claiming the model “understands”? Probing can show that information is present and used. It cannot show understanding in the human sense, because understanding is not a property you can read off a vector. Papers that keep their language precise, talking about representation and causal effect rather than beliefs and knowledge, are usually the ones to trust.

Why this matters

Probing matters because it is how we move from treating models as black boxes to treating them as systems we can inspect. But the move only works if we are honest about what each method shows. A probe result is not a mind-reading result. It is evidence that information is encoded in a readable form at a particular place. That evidence becomes powerful when combined with interventions that show the information is used, and with evaluations that show the use changes outcomes in ways we care about.

The broader lesson applies beyond probing. In interpretability, the most common mistake is not a technical one. It is a language one: upgrading a narrow measurement into a grand claim. A model does not “know” something just because a probe can predict it, just as a model does not “reason” just because it produces a plausible chain of thought. The work is in closing the gap between what we measured and what we claimed, one intervention at a time. That discipline is what will make model internals genuinely auditable, rather than merely impressive to describe.
