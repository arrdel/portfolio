Diffusion, Reasoning

Do diffusion models actually reason, or just denoise?

October 24, 2026 Adele Chinda 9 min read

Originally published on Medium on October 24, 2026. Read the original at medium.com/@chindahel1.

Autoregressive models generate by sampling from $p(x_t | x_{<t})$, one token at a time, with no mechanism for revising a token once it is committed. Discrete diffusion language models replace that process with iterative refinement: start from a fully masked sequence and repeatedly apply a learned denoiser to unmask positions in parallel. The question this raises is not rhetorical. If a model passes through dozens of intermediate states before producing its final answer, is the trajectory doing computational work that deserves the name reasoning, or is it just converging to a sample?

The mechanics, briefly

In absorbing-state discrete diffusion, the forward process progressively replaces tokens with a mask token according to a noise schedule, until the sequence is fully masked. The learned reverse process, the denoiser, predicts the clean sequence from the noisy one. Generation runs this in reverse: at each step, the model scores all masked positions, unmasks a subset (usually the highest-confidence predictions, under some unmasking policy), and optionally remasks low-confidence positions for reconsideration.

Two properties distinguish this from autoregressive decoding. First, prediction is bidirectional: a token can be conditioned on both left and right context as it emerges. Second, the process is revisable: a position unmasked at step $t$ can be remasked and changed at step $t+k$. The number of function evaluations (NFE) becomes a compute knob that autoregressive decoding does not have. More steps means more chances to revise, at the cost of more compute.

So the trajectory, the sequence of intermediate states $x_T, x_{T-1}, …, x_0$, is a real object with structure. The question is whether that structure is doing reasoning work.

The case for yes: trajectories carry information

There is evidence that intermediate states are not just noisy versions of the final answer. Three observations matter:

  1. Corrections happen. A position unmasked early with the wrong token can be remasked and corrected once surrounding context sharpens. The final answer depends on the path, not just the endpoint distribution.
  2. Compute scales with steps. Up to a point, increasing NFE improves output quality, which means later steps are adding information rather than merely resampling.
  3. Unmasking policy matters. Which positions you unmask, and in what order, changes both accuracy and efficiency. If the trajectory were epiphenomenal, the policy would not matter. Recent work on learned unmasking policies shows it matters a lot: a small learned policy can push the accuracy-per-NFE frontier outward on models like LLaDA-8B and transfer to Dream-7B.

This is the motivation behind trajectory-level evaluation: instead of grading only the final sample, study the path. Where does the model commit? Where does it revise? Which steps change the answer, and which only change the wording?

The case for no: early commitment and rationalization

The counterevidence is serious. A March 2026 study of masked diffusion models found that verdicts on reasoning tasks frequently converge very early in the trajectory, with remaining steps largely elaborating or justifying the early commitment rather than reopening it. More damning, when researchers forced longer deliberation before commitment, accuracy dropped from 86.2 percent to 71.9 percent. Forcing the model to “think longer” made it worse.

That pattern has a name in human cognition: rationalization. The system picks an answer, then spends its remaining budget making the answer look well-supported. The trajectory looks like deliberation, but the decision was already made. If the intermediate steps cannot change the outcome, they are not reasoning steps, no matter how fluent they look.

There is a second, subtler problem. In autoregressive models, chain-of-thought tokens are at least causally upstream of the answer: the answer is conditioned on them. In diffusion models, the “reasoning” is distributed across the trajectory, and there is no clean separation between the deliberation and the answer itself. The model is not thinking and then answering. It is answering continuously, and we are retroactively calling part of that process thinking.

What would settle it: interventions, not vibes

This is where the debate needs to move from interpretation to measurement. Three kinds of experiments would actually discriminate reasoning from rationalization:

  1. Trajectory interventions. Perturb an intermediate state (for example, force a different token at a position the model had settled) and see whether downstream steps recover, propagate the error, or converge to a genuinely different answer. A reasoning process should show structured recovery, not collapse or blind persistence.
  2. Step-ablation curves. Measure accuracy as a function of NFE, but per task type. If additional steps help on tasks requiring multi-step inference and hurt on tasks requiring a single judgment, that tells you something about what the steps are doing.
  3. Commitment-point analysis. Identify the step at which the final answer becomes predictable from the trajectory, and measure how much of the remaining compute changes anything. A model that commits at step 5 of 50 and then decorates is not deliberating for 45 steps.

My own view, and the direction of my current research, is that today’s diffusion LMs sit between the two poles. They do genuine revision, and unmasking policy results show the path is load-bearing. But they also commit early and rationalize, and the forced-deliberation result should worry anyone who assumes more steps equals more thought. The honest summary: diffusion models have a substrate on which reasoning could be built, and a trajectory that sometimes does real work, but we do not yet have the evaluations to say how often, or on which tasks.

Why this matters

If you are building diffusion LMs, this distinction decides where your compute goes. Scaling NFE is cheap to implement and easy to benchmark, but if most steps are rationalization, you are burning compute on decoration. The higher-leverage direction is understanding the trajectory: better unmasking policies, better remasking criteria, and evaluations that reward models whose intermediate steps actually change outcomes for the better. That is the difference between a model that looks like it reasons and one that does.
