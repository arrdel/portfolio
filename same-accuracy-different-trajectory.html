Same accuracy, different trajectory: measuring reasoning in diffusion language models

November 14, 2026 Adele Chinda 5 min read

Originally published on Medium on November 14, 2026. Read the original at medium.com/@chindahel1.

The accuracy number is not the whole story

Take two language models. Both score 88 percent on the same benchmark. Are they equally good at reasoning?

Not necessarily. One may work through the problem step by step and settle on the answer. The other may pick an answer in the first few steps and spend the rest of its output justifying a choice it already made. A third may flip between answers the whole way and happen to land on the right one at the end. Final-answer accuracy gives all three the same score.

This matters more for diffusion language models than for ordinary autoregressive models. A diffusion model does not write left to right. It starts from a fully masked answer and refines it over hundreds of denoising steps. That gives us something an autoregressive model does not: a visible trajectory of the answer forming. At every step we can ask, what does the model believe right now? When did that belief lock in? Did it ever change?

I built traj-eval to answer those questions. It is an open source toolkit that scores the trajectory, not just the final answer.

What it measures

traj-eval treats each denoising run as a sequence of verdicts, one per step, and computes a small set of interpretable quantities:

Verdict convergence step. The first step after which the verdict stops changing. A model that knows the answer immediately converges at step 3 or 4. A model that is still working converges at step 190.

Flip rate. How often the verdict changes between consecutive steps. Genuine reasoning changes its mind a few times and then settles. Oscillation flips repeatedly and never settles.

Rationalization components. Three numbers, reported separately and labeled as a heuristic, not a proof. Early factor: how much of the reasoning text appears after the verdict is already fixed. Post-change rate: how much the text keeps changing after the verdict stops changing. Length growth: how much the text grows after convergence. When all three are high, the trajectory looks like rationalization: answer first, reasoning after. The scalar score is gated, so a trajectory with no real commitment cannot score high by accident.

Commitment clocks. The step where the answer commits, the lag between commitment and stability, and how much reasoning is still unwritten when the answer converges.

One rule runs through all of it: a step with no verdict yet is recorded as no verdict, not as a change of mind. That sounds trivial, but it is the difference between measuring reasoning and measuring noise.

What we found

We ran three open checkpoints on 64 items each from ARC-Challenge and GSM8K, in two conditions: direct answering, and step-by-step reasoning with a canonical prompt.

On Dream-7B, ARC-Challenge, reasoning made accuracy worse:

Model Condition Accuracy Mean convergence step
Dream-7B Direct 0.8906 3.8
Dream-7B Reasoning 0.8281 189.5
LLaDA-8B-Instruct Direct 0.875 4.5
LLaDA-8B-Instruct Reasoning 0.9062 32.1
LLaDA-1.5 Direct 0.875 7.4
LLaDA-1.5 Reasoning 0.9219 21.7

The same intervention, the same benchmark, opposite results. That is exactly what a trajectory metric is for. On Dream, the direct answer locks in by step 4 on average, so there is almost no reasoning trajectory to measure, and asking for reasoning hurts. On LLaDA, reasoning helps, and the trajectory shows real deliberation before convergence.

On GSM8K, Dream scores 0.7656 and LLaDA scores 0.6562 on the numeric condition.

The rationalization components tell the same story from the other side, on Dream only. Direct answering has an early factor of 0.9852 and a post-change rate of 0.0001: the verdict is fixed almost immediately and the text barely changes after. Reasoning has an early factor of 0.2559 and a post-change rate of 0.1597: the verdict forms late and the text keeps developing. One of these looks like reasoning. The other looks like an answer with a justification attached.

One trajectory, two conditions

The clearest example is a single ARC-Challenge item. With step-by-step reasoning, the verdict moves from no verdict, to A, to C across the denoising steps, then stays at C. With direct answering on a comparable item, the verdict locks in at C almost immediately and never moves.

Same final answer. Completely different process. If you only record the final letter, you cannot tell them apart.

Try it

traj-eval is MIT licensed, needs only Python 3.10 and numpy, and runs a demo with no model weights: 

pip install -e .
python -m traj_eval demo

You can score your own trajectories from a JSONL file, compare direct and reasoning conditions, and get the same tables and plots. The project page has the full results and an interactive trajectory explorer:

  • Project page: https://arrdel.github.io/traj-eval/
  • Code: https://github.com/arrdel/traj-eval
  • Data and explorer: https://huggingface.co/collections/arrdel/traj-eval-diffusion-lm-reasoning-trajectories

Does it scale?

Yes, and we checked before claiming it. The framework was stress tested on the full ARC-Challenge test set (1,172 items) and the full GSM8K test set (1,319 items), plus a synthetic 10,000-trajectory file. Scoring 1,000 trajectories takes 4.3 seconds on CPU, and 10,000 takes about 34 seconds, inside a pre-registered budget of 10 minutes and 4 GB of memory. The full report is in the repo.

Honest caveats

Three things this does not claim. First, the metrics are descriptive, not causal proof. A high rationalization score means a trajectory looks like rationalization by this operational definition, not that the model was proven to rationalize. Second, the headline model numbers are 64-item suites, which are enough to show the patterns and the method, not to rank models definitively. Third, the rationalization components are reported for Dream only; the LLaDA values have not been checked against the definition and are deliberately left out.

What comes next

The next step is scale: the same measurement applied at full scale, with the trajectory as the object of study rather than the final answer. The toolkit is the first piece. If you work on diffusion language models, reasoning evaluation, or interpretability, try it and tell me where it breaks. That is the point of putting it out in the open.
