Thinking without words: verbal chain-of-thought vs latent and trajectory-based reasoning

October 27, 2026 Adele Chinda 10 min read

Originally published on Medium on October 27, 2026. Read the original at medium.com/@chindahel1.

When a language model solves a hard problem, we usually watch it think in words. It writes out steps, one token at a time, and then gives an answer. That visible reasoning, called chain-of-thought, has become the default way we judge whether a model is really reasoning. But it is not the only way a model can think. A model can also reason in its hidden states, in continuous vectors that never become words, or across the intermediate states of a diffusion trajectory that revises itself as it goes.

The question is not which style looks more like thinking. It is what each style hides, and what we lose when we can only see one of them.

Verbal chain-of-thought: thinking you can read

Verbal chain-of-thought works because it turns reasoning into generation. The model produces intermediate tokens, and each new token is conditioned on the ones before it. That gives reasoning three useful properties.

First, it is inspectable. You can read the steps, find where they go wrong, and grade them. Second, it is intervenable. You can edit a step, prompt the model to reconsider, or ask it to check its own work. Third, it is compositional in a familiar way. Steps can be reused, quoted, and checked by another model or by a person.

That readability is also the source of its main weakness. Words are a bottleneck. A model must compress whatever it computed internally into a sequence of discrete tokens, in a single order, at a fixed granularity. Some computations do not fit that shape well. A geometric intuition, a probability distribution over alternatives, or a partial result that is still uncertain may have no clean verbal form, and forcing one can distort it.

There is a deeper problem too. A verbal chain of thought is generated, not observed. The model is producing text that looks like reasoning, and the final answer is conditioned on that text, but that does not guarantee the text caused the answer in the way it appears to. Research on faithfulness has repeatedly found cases where models give the right answer for the wrong stated reason, or change their stated reasoning while keeping the answer fixed. The steps can be a rationalization written after the conclusion was already favored, rather than the path that produced it.

So verbal reasoning hides two things: the computation that did not fit into words, and the gap between the story the model tells and the computation it actually performed.

Latent and trajectory-based reasoning: thinking you cannot read

Latent reasoning skips the verbal bottleneck. Instead of writing steps, the model reasons in continuous hidden states, sometimes called latent thoughts, and only decodes an answer at the end. A related idea appears in diffusion language models. There, generation is an iterative trajectory: the model starts from a masked sequence and repeatedly refines it. The intermediate states of that trajectory are a kind of reasoning trace, but they are not sentences. They are partial answers in various stages of revision.

This style has real advantages. It avoids the cost of generating many reasoning tokens, and it avoids forcing every intermediate idea into language. A diffusion trajectory can also revise itself. A token committed early can be remasked and changed once the surrounding context sharpens, which a left to right chain of thought cannot do without starting over or adding an explicit correction step.

But what it gains in flexibility, it loses in auditability. You cannot read a hidden vector the way you read a sentence. You can probe it, decode it, or perturb it, and each of those methods tells you something, but none of them gives you the clean, checkable record that a verbal trace promises. The trajectory of a diffusion model is more inspectable than a single hidden state, since you can watch tokens appear and change, yet it still lacks a clean separation between deliberation and answer. The model is answering continuously, and we are the ones deciding which part of that process counts as thinking.

Latent reasoning therefore hides something different: the reasoning itself. When a latent model is wrong, it is harder to say where it went wrong, whether it considered an alternative, or whether an intervention would have helped. When it is right, it is harder to say why, or whether it would be right again on a slightly different problem.

The real trade-off

It is tempting to frame this as a contest, with one style winning. The more honest framing is a trade-off between expressiveness, efficiency, and accountability.

Verbal reasoning is accountable but lossy. It gives us a record we can check, at the price of forcing thought through language and trusting a record that may be partly invented.

Latent reasoning is expressive and efficient but opaque. It lets the model use its full internal representation, at the price of a record we cannot directly read.

Trajectory-based reasoning sits between the two. A diffusion trajectory is partly readable, because tokens appear and change over time, and partly opaque, because there is no labeled boundary between thinking and answering. That in-between position is why it is interesting to study. It may offer a way to keep some revisability and efficiency while recovering some inspectability, if we learn to read trajectories properly.

What would settle it

Three kinds of experiments would move this debate from preference to evidence.

First, causal interventions. Perturb a latent state, or force a different token mid-trajectory, and see whether the model recovers, propagates the error, or reaches a genuinely different answer. A reasoning process should show structured recovery, not collapse or blind persistence.

Second, decoding and probing. Train probes to read latent states, or decode them into words, and test whether the decoded content predicts the final answer better than chance, and whether editing the decoded content changes the answer. If the latent content is causally upstream of the answer, that is evidence it is doing work rather than decorating it.

Third, commitment-point analysis. Find the point in a verbal chain, a latent trajectory, or a diffusion trajectory after which the final answer becomes predictable, and measure how much of the remaining compute changes anything. A model that commits early and then decorates is not deliberating for the rest of its budget, whatever its trace looks like.

My own view is that neither style is the true form of machine reasoning, and that is the point. Verbal chains are a useful interface, not a transparent window. Latent states are a useful substrate, not a guarantee of thought. The research problem is to build evaluations that test whether intermediate computation, in whatever form, actually changes outcomes for the better, and to build methods that let us intervene on it when it does not.

Why this matters

If you are building models, this choice decides where your compute and your trust go. Scaling verbal reasoning is easy to benchmark and easy to demo, but if many of those tokens are rationalization, you are paying for decoration. Scaling latent reasoning is efficient, but if you cannot audit it, you cannot deploy it in settings where a wrong answer needs an explanation, or where a failure needs a diagnosis.

The higher-leverage direction is not to pick a side. It is to make each style more honest: verbal traces that are checked for faithfulness, latent methods that can be probed and intervened on, and trajectories whose commitment points are measured rather than assumed. A model that reasons well should be able to show its work when asked, revise its work when wrong, and do useful work that never needed words in the first place. We do not yet have all three in one system, and closing that gap is the interesting problem.
